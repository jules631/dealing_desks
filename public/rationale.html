Product Rationale

Why Deal Desk AI exists

Author: Product Team Status: Working prototype Last updated: Q2 2025

The problem we're solving

Enterprise software sales runs on conversations. An AE has a call, a Slack thread, a few emails. Inside those conversations is everything you need to price and justify a deal: company size, budget signals, timeline pressure, competitive context, who has authority. All of it is there. Almost none of it makes it into the CPQ.

Current CPQ tools — Salesforce CPQ, DealHub, Logik.ai — are form-first. They start with fields. The AE has to translate the conversation into those fields manually, usually after the fact, usually in a hurry. The translation is lossy by design.

"AEs lose hours translating conversation context into CPQ configuration, and still submit quotes that get rejected because the approval justification doesn't reflect the actual deal conversation."

Two problems compound each other: the quote is often misconfigured (wrong product tier, wrong pricing model, wrong volume band), and the justification submitted to deal desk is generic boilerplate that doesn't reflect the specific conversation signals that make the discount defensible. The result: revision cycles, rejected approvals, and deals that slip because the internal process took longer than the prospect's decision window.

Why this is solvable now

The core capability that makes this tractable is a language model that can read a raw, unstructured conversation and extract structured deal intelligence — not just key-phrase matching, but genuine understanding of what's explicit versus implied, what constitutes a budget signal versus a preference, and what level of confidence to assign each extraction.

Claude, in particular, has two properties that make it the right model for this use case:

  • Calibrated confidence: It distinguishes between what the prospect said directly and what can be inferred. This maps naturally to the Explicit / Implied signal taxonomy we use.
  • Voice-aware generation: The approval justification it drafts sounds like an experienced AE wrote it — not a summarization tool. It's specific to the signals, not generic to the deal type.

The gap existing tools leave

Tool What it does What it misses
Salesforce CPQ Product catalog + pricing rules + approval workflows Starts with fields. No conversation layer. Justification is AE-authored from scratch.
DealHub Guided selling + document generation Guided by rules, not conversation. Outputs are templated, not grounded in the specific deal.
Logik.ai Complex product configuration logic Solves configuration complexity, not the translation from conversation to configuration.
Gong / Chorus Call recording + coaching insights Surfaces conversation intelligence retrospectively. Does not produce a quote or justification.
Deal Desk AI Conversation → structured quote → approval justification —

The white space is the translation layer. Every AE does this manually today. It's a 45-minute task that produces mediocre output and leaves value — both in the quote configuration and in the justification quality — on the table.

Key design decisions

1. Conversation-first, not field-first

The input is a textarea, not a form. This is deliberate. The AE's native artifact is the conversation — an email thread, call notes, a forwarded Slack message. Making them re-encode that into fields before we can help them defeats the purpose. We read the conversation. We produce the structure.

2. Explicit vs. Implied confidence taxonomy

Not all signals are equal. "Budget is $18k/month, approved" is different from "they seem budget-constrained." The distinction matters for how much weight the justification can put on each signal. We surface this explicitly so the AE can decide how to represent the deal — we don't hide uncertainty inside confident-sounding prose.

3. Grounded justifications, not generated ones

The approval justification must be traceable to specific conversation signals. "Customer has a Q2 deadline" is only useful if the conversation actually contained a Q2 deadline. We instruct the model to never invent signals — and to write justifications that cite the source of each claim. Deal desk analysts are experienced at spotting boilerplate. We don't produce boilerplate.

4. Discount risk before commitment

The discount risk assessment surfaces before the AE submits. This matters because the cost of getting a VP approval flag wrong isn't just the rejected approval — it's the week of back-and-forth, the deal that cools down, the rep whose credibility takes a hit. Showing the gap between what the prospect implied and what policy allows gives the AE a decision to make, not a surprise to manage.

5. Deal desk simulation as a rehearsal mechanism

The "Simulate Deal Desk Review" feature runs a second AI pass acting as a skeptical analyst. The pushback questions it returns are the questions a deal desk analyst would actually ask — not generic challenges, but challenges specific to this deal's signals and justification. This is the difference between submitting something that holds up and submitting something that bounces back on day three of a Q-end week.

What we are not building

  • A CPQ replacement. We generate a quote configuration recommendation. The AE still submits through whatever CPQ the org uses. We're the layer before CPQ, not a replacement for it.
  • A call recording tool. We process text the AE provides. We don't record calls, transcribe audio, or integrate with Gong. The AE controls what context they feed us.
  • An approval automation tool. We draft the justification. A human still submits it and a human still approves it. We're making the human's input better, not removing them from the loop.
  • A CRM data sync. No Salesforce integration in v1. The AE copies the justification to their clipboard and pastes it where it needs to go. This is a feature, not a limitation — it keeps the tool lightweight and the AE in control.

The product catalog model

The current catalog contains three products. Each has seat-based and consumption-based pricing options. The AI recommends a pricing model based on signals from the conversation:

Product Seat Price Consumption Price Max Discount Best fit
WorkflowCore $45 / seat / mo $0.08 / workflow-run 25% Process automation pain
AnalyticsPlus $30 / seat / mo $0.12 / report-generated 20% Reporting / data visibility pain
AIAgentSuite $120 / seat / mo $0.45 / agent-action 15% (VP flag above 10%) AI automation / headcount reduction goals

The consumption vs. seat recommendation follows a simple rule: if the prospect expresses uncertainty about headcount, mentions variable usage, or describes seasonal spikes, consumption is recommended. If headcount is stable and budget is predictable, seat-based wins. A "mixed transition deal" flag is raised if the prospect is moving from seat to consumption — this requires revenue ops review.

How we'd measure success

  • Time from call to quote submission. Baseline is approximately 45–90 minutes. Target is under 10 minutes for the extraction and configuration pass.
  • First-pass approval rate. Quotes that are approved without a revision request. We'd measure this against a control group using the standard workflow.
  • Justification reuse rate. The percentage of submitted justifications where the AE used the AI draft with minimal editing. High reuse means the output is actually useful; low reuse means we're generating a starting point, not a finished artifact.
  • Deal velocity. Time from quote submission to closed-won. Hypothesis: faster, better-justified quotes close faster because they don't stall in approval.

The bet we're making

The bet is that the conversation is the source of truth for the deal, and that extracting structure from it — rather than asking the AE to encode structure into forms — produces better outcomes: more accurate quotes, more defensible justifications, fewer revision cycles, faster closes.

CPQ tools have solved the configuration problem. They haven't solved the translation problem. That's the gap Deal Desk AI is built to close.

The product is conversation-first by conviction, not by convenience. Every design decision flows from that single constraint.
